Africa should invest more in satellite remote sensing data skills

In 1962, newly independent African states came together to build an elite statistics school that still runs their economies. The same idea today could turn free satellite data into valuable information that the continent needs to face its challenges.

YA
Yves Atchadé
Boston University  ·  7 min read  ·  November 2026

Statistics is crucial for governance and political stability. At their independence around 1960, very few African countries had the technical statistical capacity to govern. To the credit of the founding fathers, the statistics apparatus was one of the first they decided to build. In 1962 the newly independent sub-Saharan francophone states, France’s INSEE and the six-member European Economic Community jointly created the CESD (Centre Européen de Formation des Statisticiens-Économistes des pays en voie de développement) to train French-speaking African statisticians. It was housed inside ENSAE, France’s elite statistics school, and its students took roughly 80% of ENSAE’s courses. The highest degree of the program is the Ingénieur Statisticien Économiste (ISE), roughly equivalent to a master’s degree in econometrics in the university system. The training then progressively moved to Africa — to Abidjan, Yaoundé, Dakar and Kigali — and the CESD stopped teaching in 1994. The school in Abidjan, founded in 1961 as the École de statistique d’Abidjan and renamed ENSEA in 1982, would become the flagship.

The result of this ambitious program was one of the most quietly successful institutional experiments of the independence era. In 1977 the United Nations counted every professional statistician working in Africa. Leaving aside Egypt, South Africa and a handful of territories still under colonial rule, the total was estimated at 1,482 people. Add the middle-level staff and the number reached about 4,460 for the whole continent. According to the same survey, the CESD had trained 499 professional and ISE (that is roughly one-third of the total), and 352 middle-level statisticians. The impact of the program is also clearly visible today. The ISEs essentially run the economic measurement apparatus of francophone Africa. The current heads of the statistical institutes in Guinea, Senegal, Togo and Côte d’Ivoire are ISE holders. AFRISTAT — the West Africa intergovernmental statistical body — was founded and first directed by Lamine Diop, an ISE of the CESD who had earlier run the CESD itself, the first African to hold that post. Many in the senior leadership of the administrations and private sectors of these countries are also ISEs.

The key mechanism that made the success of the program is still running today. It is a jointly held, fiercely competitive entrance examination (“concours”) — first established in 1963 — that aims to recruit students with talent in mathematics, and train them in statistics. The competitive entrance exam attracts talent, makes admission meritocratic and therefore politically hard to corrupt. I went through the program some 30 years ago, and I remember being deeply fascinated by its pursuit of excellence.

What governments need to know has changed

With growing pressure on natural resources and the environment, the information that African governments need today to govern has drastically changed. What will the agricultural output of the country be in a given year? What is the flooding risk profile across the country, particularly in peri-urban settlements? Very few African countries have serious answers to this type of questions. According to the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), the continent has the world’s least developed land-based weather observation network, and around 60% of its 1.2 billion people are not covered by any early warning system. Meanwhile, according to the same study, African countries lose an average of 2–5% of GDP a year to climate extremes and divert as much as 9% of their budgets responding to them.

The technology to acquire all of this information exists, it is in orbit, and the data is mostly free. The Landsat archive (an open archive operated by NASA) alone generated an estimated $25.6 billion of value to users worldwide in 2023, according to an estimate produced by the US Geological Survey. Similarly for Sentinel, and a dozen of other programs. Over 65 African satellites have been launched, more than twenty African states run space programs, and the African Space Agency was inaugurated in Cairo on 20 April 2025. In short, the remote sensing data is free and abundant. The demand for the information it carries is enormous, measurable in points of GDP. What is crucially needed today is, exactly as it was in 1961, data specialists to convert this data into information.

The diagnosis in Paris was right

On 9 and 10 September 2026 France convened the first International Space Summit at the Grand Palais, with heads of state, ministers and delegations from around 120 countries and organizations. The African diagnosis delivered there was accurate. Rwanda’s Paul Kagame told the hall that Africa intends to be “a full participant in this growth — not only as a user of space capabilities, but also as a contributor to science, research and global decision-making”. The DRC’s Félix Tshisekedi was blunter:

“An Africa that depends entirely on others to observe its territory, communicate, manage its data or anticipate its crises remains a vulnerable Africa.” Félix Tshisekedi, International Space Summit, Paris, September 2026

He said explicitly that the states responsible for the Congo Basin need not only satellite data but the capacity to process it, alongside trained scientists, engineers and entrepreneurs. However, the action plans proposed in response lack the much-needed investment to address the human capital challenge. Nigeria came to Paris seeking more than $1 billion of investment for six new satellites, a propulsion center and a domestic rocket program. The DRC proposed a Congo Basin space partnership and a regional observatory. African governments have budgeted about $828 million for space in 2026, a third more than the year before, but mostly for hardware.

Controlling the data acquisition with new national satellites is certainly important as a matter of national sovereignty, but is not much progress if the data processing is sub-contracted to foreign entities. African countries must strategically pool their effort and massively invest to train remote sensing data experts. Satellite remote sensing is a modern triumph of science and technology. A marriage of mathematics, physics, engineering, signal processing and computer science. Unfortunately there is, on the African continent, no remote sensing equivalent of the ISE profile: no three-year, fiercely selective engineering degree recruiting out of serious mathematics preparation, that trains the students in physics, electronics and engineering, statistics, hardware and database technologies, and whose graduates are expected to invent methods rather than apply them.

The schools that exist, and the tier they teach to

To be clear, there are several serious regional geospatial information science schools on the continent. The Regional Centre for Training in Aerospace Surveys was founded at Ile-Ife in 1972 under the auspices of the UN Economic Commission for Africa. Now renamed AFRIGIST, it has trained more than 3,800 people since inception, according to its website. The Regional Centre for Mapping of Resources for Development has operated from Nairobi since 1975 and has twenty member states. Closest of all to what I am proposing is CRASTE-LF, the UN-affiliated African regional center for space science and technology in the French language, housed at the École Mohammed V d’Ingénieurs in Rabat. It runs a two-year master’s in space science and technology, with tracks in remote sensing and GIS and in satellite meteorology, degreed jointly with Université Mohammed V and open to candidates from thirteen member states, from Cape Verde to the DRC.

These institutions do real work and deserve more money than they get. The question is not whether they teach well but what tier they teach to. And this question is not about the degree they deliver. AFRIGIST’s portfolio runs from an 18-month technologist diploma up to professional masters and a PhD track; CRASTE-LF awards a recognized master’s. The levels go high enough. What differs is the aim, and it shows first in who gets in. Entry at AFRIGIST and CRASTE-LF is by application review with preference for serving civil servants. The intake in these schools is predominantly from professionals already in post, not from the strongest young mathematicians and physicists on the continent, and the syllabus is organized around producing geospatial information rather than around the physics, the signal processing and the computing theory that produce this geospatial information in the first place. Graduates of these programs are not trained to invent better algorithms for the environments they work in.

How to do it again

This is precisely what has made the success of the CESD experiment. The CESD has offered several professional degrees, but has also pushed to get the hardest, most expensive tier — the ISE cycle. That top tier is where the innovation and the senior leadership come from. The CESD experiment also shows that with political will, this type of massive capacity building can be done. Today there is no need to anchor such a program to France or any other colonial power. But several factors are important to make it work.

Keep the concours. One of the key successes of the CESD model is that entrance in the program is based on the ranking from a rigorous entrance examination (that emphasizes mathematics), simultaneously administered in all participating countries. Discarding this feature as a technicality would be a mistake. The power of the concours to attract very talented young students cannot be overstated.

Mandate the reporting of agrometeorological and environmental statistics. One effective way to accelerate the use of satellite remote sensing data is to create national remote sensing data institutes, and mandate at the national and regional levels the production of official and harmonized agrometeorological and environmental statistics. The institutes are de facto potential employers, and in turn their researchers become future instructors in the school.

The diagnosis of the African leadership at the International Space Summit is accurate. However, the proposed plans lack the long-term commitment to build the needed brainpower to convert remote sensing data into information on the continent. The CESD program shows that (and how) it can be done.
